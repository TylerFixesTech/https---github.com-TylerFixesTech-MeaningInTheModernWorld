Nineteenth-Century Music Analyses

Five compositions from Romanticism to a later Impressionist transition

By Tyler Tinney

Music reveals the emotions, structures, and values of an era through rhythm, harmony, instrumentation, texture, and expression. These selections are analyzed as musical works, not embedded commercial recordings. The original research and listening references are linked from the sources page.

Listening analysis 01 • Romanticism

Franz Schubert — Erlkönig (1815)

Romantic & late-century music

Franz Schubert's Erlkönig (1815) is a German art song, or Lied, written for solo singers and one piano-player, singing the poem of the same name written by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe. In its story, a father speeds forward in the night carrying his frightened child, who believes the supernatural Erlking is trying to take him. The pianist plays rapid, repeated figures that evoke the horse's gallop and create tension before the singer has even explained the danger. One singer usually portrays the narrator, father, son, and Erlking, distinguishing them through changes in register, tone, and expression. The father's steady reassurances contrast with the child's mounting panic and the Erlking's deceptively gentle appeals. Changes in dynamics and harmony make the increasingly desperate situation audible, and the ending becomes especially chilling when the motion stops. Schubert's music does more than accompany a poem: it makes the piano an active storyteller. This is characteristic of Romantic music because it unites literature, supernatural fear, and intense personal emotion in one dramatic performance. [11]

Listening analysis 02 • Romanticism

Richard Wagner — Ride of the Valkyries (Die Walküre, 1870 premiere)

Romantic & late-century music

Richard Wagner's Ride of the Valkyries is the famous opening of Act III of his opera Die Walküre, first staged in 1870. Its full theatrical medium includes orchestra and the voices of the Valkyries, although concert performances often present an orchestral excerpt alone. The scene depicts mythological warrior women riding through the air and gathering fallen mortal warriors, so Wagner needed music that could suggest movement, danger, and supernatural power. Swirling strings and woodwinds create a restless background while a bold brass motif rises above the orchestra. Repetition, strong rhythmic drive, and swelling dynamics build the sense of a force that cannot easily be stopped. The music can sound triumphant, but it is also connected to battle and the deaths of warriors, which complicates a purely heroic interpretation. Its enormous orchestral sound demonstrates Wagner's interest in combining drama, mythology, and music into a complete theatrical experience. For the Romantic era, the piece illustrates the appeal of legendary subjects and the ability of music to make an imaginary scene feel immediate and overwhelming. [12]

Listening analysis 03 • Romanticism

Hector Berlioz — March to the Scaffold (Symphonie fantastique, 1830)

Romantic & late-century music

March to the Scaffold is the fourth movement of Hector Berlioz's orchestral Symphonie fantastique, first performed in 1830. It is program music, meaning that the instrumental composition follows a described story instead of relying on lyrics. In Berlioz's narrative, a young artist dreams that he has murdered the woman he loves and is being led to his execution. The movement's heavy, recurring march rhythm and emphatic percussion give the imagined procession a sense of grim inevitability. Changes between ominous passages and louder, almost celebratory brass and orchestral outbursts suggest a disturbing public spectacle. Just before the end, a brief appearance of the recurring melody representing the beloved, called the idée fixe, interrupts the march before a violent conclusion. The contrast makes obsession and romantic love sound inseparable from terror and punishment. Berlioz's imaginative orchestration and intensely personal narrative demonstrate how Romantic composers expanded the symphony into a form of psychological storytelling. [13]

Listening analysis 04 • Romanticism

Frédéric Chopin — Polonaise in A-flat Major, Op. 53, “Heroic” (1842–1843)

Romantic & late-century music

Frédéric Chopin's Polonaise in A-flat Major, Op. 53, commonly nicknamed the Heroic Polonaise, is a large-scale work for solo piano composed in 1842–1843. The polonaise originated as a Polish dance, and Chopin transformed its characteristic triple-meter rhythm into music designed for the concert hall. After a forceful introduction, broad chords and a proud, rhythmic main theme create an impression of dignity and determination. The demanding middle section features repeated notes, energetic motion, and a sense of gathering momentum. A quieter contrasting passage makes the return of the main theme sound especially powerful. Because Chopin was a Polish composer living abroad during a time of political upheaval in his homeland, the piece can reasonably be heard in relation to Polish cultural pride, although it does not tell a literal battle story. Its virtuoso technique is essential to the emotional message rather than merely an opportunity for the pianist to show off. The work is a strong example of Romanticism because it transforms a national tradition into an intensely individual expression of grandeur, struggle, and resolve. [14]

Listening analysis 05 • Impressionist transition

Claude Debussy — Clair de lune (Suite bergamasque; early version c. 1890, published 1905)

Romantic & late-century music

Claude Debussy's Clair de lune is the third movement of his Suite bergamasque, a work for solo piano that originated around 1890 and was revised before publication in 1905. That publication date falls just outside the nineteenth century, so the piece is best discussed here as a bridge from late-Romantic expression toward twentieth-century musical modernism. Its title means 'moonlight' and recalls the poetic atmosphere associated with Paul Verlaine. Rather than describing a sequence of dramatic events, Debussy uses delicate dynamics, gently shifting harmonies, flexible phrasing, and flowing arpeggios to suggest an elusive mood. The piece begins quietly, grows into a fuller and more luminous middle section, and gradually settles back into stillness. Its texture often makes the melody seem to emerge from and disappear into the surrounding harmonies. The result is both intimate and spacious, allowing the listener to interpret the moonlit atmosphere personally. Compared with Berlioz's violent march, Clair de lune shows how music associated with Impressionism can prioritize changing color and sensation over an explicit narrative. [15]
