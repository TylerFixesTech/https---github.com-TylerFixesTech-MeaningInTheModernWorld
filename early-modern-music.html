Early Modern Music Analyses

Six works from Baroque and Classical traditions

By Tyler Tinney

Music reveals the emotions, structures, and values of an era through rhythm, harmony, instrumentation, texture, and expression. These selections are analyzed as musical works, not embedded commercial recordings. The original research and listening references are linked from the sources page.

Listening analysis 01 • Baroque

“Tu se’ morta” from L’Orfeo

Claudio Monteverdi • 1607 • Baroque • Operatic recitative (Act II)

In 'Tu se’ morta' from L’Orfeo, the character Orpheus learns that his beloved Eurydice has died and struggles to respond. Rather than a steady dance rhythm, the singer uses a speechlike recitative whose pacing follows the emotional shape of the Italian words. The accompaniment is spare, supported by basso continuo, which leaves the solo voice exposed and intensifies the feeling of grief. Short melodic motions, pauses, and expressive dissonances reflect shock and uncertainty, while the harmony often takes on a dark, minor-key character. As Orpheus resolves to follow Eurydice toward the underworld, the line becomes more purposeful, suggesting determination emerging from despair. These changes are examples of musical rhetoric and word painting, where sound reinforces particular feelings or phrases in the text. The texture is primarily one melody supported by chords, making every inflection of the soloist significant. Monteverdi's music demonstrates how early opera used dramatic text and musical expression together to make a mythological story feel intensely human.

Listening analysis 02 • Baroque

“Spring,” First Movement, from The Four Seasons

Antonio Vivaldi • composed circa 1718–1720; published 1725 • Baroque • Violin concerto in E major, RV 269

Vivaldi's first movement of 'Spring' begins with a bright, fast Allegro theme that suggests the excitement of nature awakening. A solo violin alternates with the string ensemble and basso continuo, creating a strong contrast between individual display and the returning orchestral ritornello. High trills and quick notes imitate birds calling, while gentle, flowing figures evoke streams and breezes. The mood darkens when repeated notes and agitated passages suggest thunder and lightning, including a temporary move toward minor harmony. After the storm, the cheerful material and birdlike gestures return, giving the movement a sense of renewal. Clear changes in dynamics, register, and texture help make the imagined outdoor scene understandable even without sung words. The concerto is program music because it follows ideas expressed in an accompanying poem, and the instrumental descriptions are often called tone painting. Its energetic rhythmic drive and elaborate solo violin writing make nature sound playful, unpredictable, and ultimately joyful.

Listening analysis 03 • Baroque

“Hallelujah” Chorus from Messiah

George Frideric Handel • composed 1741; premiered with Messiah in 1742 • Baroque • Sacred oratorio, HWV 56

Handel's 'Hallelujah' Chorus celebrates divine kingship through the combined sound of a four-part choir and orchestra. Its energetic opening and repeated exclamations establish a triumphant major-key atmosphere. The composer alternates passages in which the singers move together rhythmically with more intricate passages where different voice parts enter in succession. These changes between homophonic and imitative polyphonic textures make the proclamation feel both unified and expansive. Short repeated phrases build intensity, especially where the voices declare that the reign will last forever. Strong rhythmic accents and full-ensemble passages heighten the feeling of communal celebration, while softer or thinner moments make later arrivals sound more powerful. Because the text is biblical, its meaning is reinforced by direct musical gestures rather than by a plot acted out onstage. The work shows how Baroque composers could combine dramatic musical techniques with public religious expression in a large-scale sacred composition.

Listening analysis 04 • Classical

“Eine kleine Nachtmusik,” First Movement

Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart • 1787 • Classical • G major, K. 525, Allegro

Mozart's first movement of Eine kleine Nachtmusik opens with a bold, memorable string statement that immediately establishes an energetic but orderly mood. The music is scored for strings, and the bright leading melody is supported by accompanying parts that make the texture easy to follow. Its brisk Allegro tempo and symmetrical phrases reveal the Classical preference for clarity and balance. Contrasting themes provide variety before familiar musical ideas return, illustrating the structural logic associated with sonata form. Small shifts between loud and soft, together with precise rhythmic patterns, sustain excitement without creating the continuous thick texture often heard in Baroque music. Although the title refers to night music, this movement does not attempt to imitate a literal nighttime scene. Instead, the polished exchanges and well-organized themes suggest social entertainment, elegance, and confident craftsmanship. The movement offers a useful comparison with Vivaldi because both are lively, but Mozart emphasizes balanced formal development more than pictorial descriptions of nature.

Listening analysis 05 • Classical

“Surprise” Symphony, Second Movement

Joseph Haydn • composed 1791 • Classical • Symphony No. 94 in G major, Andante

The second movement of Haydn's Symphony No. 94 begins with a gentle, regularly phrased melody that seems almost like a simple song. The soft string texture creates a comfortable expectation, but a sudden loud chord from the orchestra interrupts it and gives the symphony its famous 'Surprise' nickname. This unexpected dynamic contrast is humorous because it breaks a pattern the listener has just learned. Afterward, Haydn repeats and reshapes the musical theme through variations that change accompaniment, instrumentation, register, and intensity. The moderate Andante pace keeps the central tune recognizable even as its surroundings become more elaborate. Balanced phrases and clear tonal organization reflect Classical style, while the abrupt punctuation shows the composer's playful imagination. The orchestra's shifts between delicate and powerful sounds make active listening part of the experience. Haydn demonstrates that a carefully ordered structure can still be full of wit, inventiveness, and dramatic surprise.

Listening analysis 06 • Classical (early Beethoven)

“Adagio cantabile” from Piano Sonata No. 8, “Pathétique”

Ludwig van Beethoven • composed 1797–1799 • Classical (early Beethoven) • Op. 13, second movement

The second movement of Beethoven's 'Pathétique' Sonata presents a slow, singing melody above a quiet, flowing piano accompaniment. Its Adagio cantabile indication asks for a gentle pace and a lyrical quality that contrasts with the more turbulent character of the sonata's first movement. The opening theme returns several times, separated by contrasting episodes, giving the piece a rounded, reassuring structure. Changes in register, harmony, and accompaniment keep the melody expressive without losing its identity. A more unsettled central passage briefly darkens the mood before the familiar theme restores a sense of calm. The texture often resembles a singer supported by an accompaniment, even though only one pianist performs the entire movement. Instead of the obvious theatrical gestures of Baroque opera, Beethoven communicates emotional depth through restrained phrasing and carefully judged harmonic color. This piece suggests how late-eighteenth-century Classical conventions could also support the personal intensity that would become prominent in later music.
