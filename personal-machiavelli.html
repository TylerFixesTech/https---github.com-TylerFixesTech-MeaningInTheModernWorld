A personal reflection on leadership, strength, and accountability

By Tyler Tinney

Machiavelli on Love and Fear

Machiavelli presents leadership as a practical responsibility in which a ruler sometimes has to choose effectiveness over qualities that would normally be considered virtuous. He argues that the ideal leader would be both loved and feared, but because those qualities are difficult to maintain together, it is safer to be feared when a choice must be made (Machiavelli, Chapter XVII). However, he is equally clear that a leader should avoid becoming hated, since hatred can undermine authority and encourage opposition. Machiavelli also believes that punishment or harshness can sometimes be justified when it prevents greater disorder, using Cesare Borgia as an example of a ruler whose severity restored order. At the same time, qualities such as generosity and mercy cannot simply be practiced without considering their consequences, because an apparently virtuous action can sometimes weaken a ruler or harm the state. His approach therefore emphasizes results and prudence rather than following a single moral rule in every situation. He makes a similar argument about keeping promises, stating that a ruler may sometimes need to break his word when circumstances have changed or when others cannot be trusted to keep theirs. Overall, Machiavelli views effective leadership as a balance of strength, caution, reputation, and adaptability, with the leader always needing to consider how his actions affect his ability to govern.

I have encountered leaders who illustrate both sides of Machiavelli's argument about love, fear, and respect. Some leaders I knew when I was younger seemed so concerned with being liked that they were unwilling to firmly correct people or enforce expectations. As a result, other members of the group often acted out, ignored instructions, and stopped taking those leaders seriously, demonstrating Machiavelli's warning that appearing weak or irresolute can cause a leader to be despised. In contrast, some of my best teachers were willing to sacrifice being universally liked in order to maintain order and accomplish the learning they were responsible for providing. Even some of the most unruly students understood the boundaries in those classrooms, while the teachers developed reputations for being strict but fair rather than cruel. I have also seen a similar contrast in politics between former Alberta Premier Jason Kenney and his successor, Danielle Smith. In my experience and observation, Kenney often spoke strongly about defending Alberta's interests but appeared too willing to compromise when those positions were challenged, and I personally became involved with members of his party who opposed his leadership. Smith, by contrast, has been more willing to openly challenge federal policies that she believes conflict with Alberta's interests, demonstrating the kind of decisiveness and willingness to accept criticism that Machiavelli associates with stronger leadership.

From these examples, I believe Machiavelli's most useful lesson is not that leaders should deliberately make people afraid of them, but that leaders cannot allow the desire to be liked to become more important than their responsibilities. The weaker leaders I observed often gained neither love nor respect because people eventually recognized that their words carried no real consequences. The stronger teachers were more effective because expectations were clear, discipline was consistent, and the people under their leadership knew where they stood. Machiavelli's warning that a ruler should be feared without becoming hated is especially important because authority without fairness can easily turn into resentment rather than respect. As a disciple leader, however, I would not want fear to be the foundation of my leadership in my home, community, or Church service. Instead, I can apply the useful part of Machiavelli's principle by combining genuine Christlike love with the courage to correct problems, establish boundaries, and make difficult decisions when necessary. I have learned that generosity and kindness are most effective when they are joined with consistency, accountability, and a willingness to stand for principles even when doing so may be unpopular. My goal as a disciple leader would therefore be to become someone people can trust and respect because I care about them while still having enough courage to lead when leadership requires difficult choices.
