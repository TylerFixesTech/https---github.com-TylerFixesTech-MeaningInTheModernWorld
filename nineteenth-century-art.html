Nineteenth-Century Art Analyses

Seven works from Romanticism through the late nineteenth century

By Tyler Tinney

Art is another way to understand the ideas and experiences of this period. The selections below move through different artists and movements, with each analysis considering subject matter, technique, and historical meaning. Some reproductions are already linked from verified open sources; other entries have clearly marked image spaces until an appropriate file is selected.

Artwork 01 • Romanticism

The Raft of the Medusa (1818–1819)

Théodore Géricault

Théodore Géricault's The Raft of the Medusa (1818–1819) is a monumental oil painting on canvas inspired by the true and gruesome story of a French frigate in 1816. Around 150 people were abandoned by their leaders on a cobbled together raft, and only a mere fifteen people managed to make it to safety. Géricault organized the figures in rising diagonal and triangular groupings, leading the viewer's eye from the dead and exhausted toward survivors signaling a distant ship. The dark sea, threatening sky, and strongly modeled bodies intensify the desperation of the moment. The painting debuted in the Paris Salon of 1819, where its unsettling subject and political implications divided critics. Rather than celebrating a conventional heroic victory, it confronts viewers with human suffering and the consequences of failed leadership. The tiny hope of rescue on the horizon adds emotional tension because the audience cannot immediately tell whether the survivors will be seen. This work represents Romanticism through its dramatic scale, historical urgency, emotional extremes, and willingness to challenge comfortable ideas about authority. [16]

Artwork 02 • Romanticism

The Fighting Temeraire (1839)

J. M. W. Turner

J. M. W. Turner's The Fighting Temeraire (1839) is an oil painting showing a veteran British warship being towed toward dismantling by a steam-powered tug. The Temeraire had fought at Trafalgar in 1805, making it a symbol of Britain's naval past. Turner paints the old ship in pale, almost ghostly colors while the small tug appears dark and forceful against the glowing sunset. The dramatic sky and mirrored light give the scene a solemn, almost ceremonial character. The image is deliberately idealized: the historical ship had already lost much of its rigging, but Turner restored its stately appearance. Critics praised it when it first appeared at the Royal Academy in 1839, recognizing its poetic and patriotic feeling. The contrast between sail and steam suggests the passing of an older age and the uncertain emotions attached to industrial progress, without proving that Turner simply opposed technology. Its combination of atmosphere, memory, national identity, and change makes it an important Romantic response to modernization. [17]

Artwork 03 • Realism

The Gleaners (1857)

Jean-François Millet

Jean-François Millet's The Gleaners (1857) is an oil painting on canvas depicting three rural women bending to collect leftover grain after the main harvest. The women dominate the foreground while a much larger and more abundant agricultural operation fills the distant background. Millet repeats their bent poses to emphasize the physically exhausting and repetitive nature of work that brought little reward. Warm light lends the laborers dignity, but the contrast between their meager collection and the wealthy harvest points toward economic inequality. A mounted supervisor in the background also hints at the distance between workers and those who control the land. Some contemporary viewers found the prominence given to poor peasants troubling, particularly in an era concerned about class conflict. Millet does not turn the women into glamorous heroes or use spectacular events to make his point; he asks the audience to look carefully at ordinary lives. The painting therefore exemplifies Realism and shows how nineteenth-century artists increasingly made working people worthy subjects of serious art. [18]

Artwork 04 • Impressionism

Impression, Sunrise (1872)

Claude Monet

Claude Monet's Impression, Sunrise (1872) is an oil painting on canvas depicting the harbor of Le Havre at daybreak. The port's cranes, boats, and smokestacks dissolve into gray-blue mist, while the small orange sun and its reflection provide the strongest color contrast. Monet uses loose, visible brushstrokes rather than outlining each ship or building precisely, making changing light and atmosphere the real subject. The painting was included in an independent exhibition in 1874, and its title helped inspire the initially critical label 'Impressionists.' Some viewers regarded the apparently unfinished handling as a rejection of traditional artistic standards. Yet the scene is carefully organized to reproduce how a particular place might appear during a fleeting moment. The working harbor also quietly acknowledges the industrial world that had become part of everyday life. This makes the painting an essential example of Impressionism: the artist values perception and transient visual experience more than polished detail or a grand historical narrative. [19]

Artwork 05 • Impressionism

Bal du moulin de la Galette (1876)

Pierre-Auguste Renoir

Pierre-Auguste Renoir's Bal du moulin de la Galette (1876) is a large oil painting of a lively outdoor social gathering in the Montmartre neighborhood of Paris. Groups of friends sit, talk, and dance, creating a scene of recreation rather than war, mythology, or noble history. Renoir paints patches of shifting light across faces, clothing, and the ground, making the crowded setting feel lively and immediate. His bright colors and loosely defined contours suggest people constantly moving rather than posing for a formal portrait. Some figures are based on people he knew, but the collective mood matters more than any single individual. Shown at the Impressionist exhibition of 1877, the painting drew criticism for its blurred handling while demonstrating the group's interest in contemporary life. It captures a side of the modern city where ordinary people could find pleasure, sociability, and moments of relief from work. The work is a hallmark of Impressionism because it treats passing light and everyday urban experience as subjects worthy of major art. [20]

Artwork 06 • Post-Impressionism

The Bedroom (1888)

Vincent van Gogh

Vincent van Gogh's The Bedroom (1888) is an oil painting of his simple room in the Yellow House at Arles, France. The furniture is ordinary, but the tilted perspective, bold outlines, and strong contrasts of color immediately make the room feel unlike an objective architectural record. Van Gogh explained that the painting was intended to suggest rest or sleep, a purpose that may surprise viewers who find its colors intense. The doors, bed, chairs, and pictures are simplified into large visual forms, showing his interest in the expressive possibilities of color and design. The museum notes that some pigments have changed over time, so the room originally had different color relationships from those visible today. Van Gogh also deliberately reduced shadows and flattened space in ways influenced by Japanese prints. This approach turns an unremarkable personal interior into an emotional self-expression rather than a straightforward record. The work is characteristic of Post-Impressionism because it builds on modern color and brushwork while giving subjective meaning greater importance than optical accuracy. [21]

Artwork 07 • Expressionism

The Scream (1893)

Edvard Munch

Edvard Munch's The Scream exists in several versions; the well-known 1893 painting held by Norway's National Museum uses tempera and crayon on cardboard. A pale figure stands on a bridge with hands pressed against its head while the sky and surrounding landscape bend into sweeping, unsettling curves. The two distant figures continue along the path, increasing the central figure's apparent isolation. The strong orange and blue contrasts make the environment seem to participate in the figure's anxiety rather than remain a neutral background. The open mouth and distorted face communicate fear in a way that does not depend on realistic anatomical detail. Munch's writings about the subject connect it to a disturbing sensation of a scream in nature, so the work represents inner experience as much as any outward event. It is usually associated with Symbolism and is an important precursor to Expressionism rather than a straightforward Impressionist painting. The Scream closes the century with an unsettling reminder that artistic progress also involved discovering new ways to represent loneliness, dread, and the human mind. [22]
