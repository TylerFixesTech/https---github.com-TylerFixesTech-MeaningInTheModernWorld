Justice, Mercy and The Tempest

A personal reflection on Prospero and forgiveness

By Tyler Tinney

Personal connection essay

Justice, Mercy and The Tempest

In The Tempest, Shakespeare presents mercy as something Prospero ultimately chooses even though he has strong reasons to seek justice against those who wronged him. Antonio took Prospero’s position as Duke of Milan with Alonso’s support, while Sebastian later demonstrates similar corruption when he joins Antonio in plotting against Alonso. Prospero spends much of the play using his power to confront these men with the consequences of their actions rather than immediately taking revenge against them. Alonso eventually shows regret for what happened and agrees to restore Prospero’s dukedom, which makes Prospero’s forgiveness of him feel connected to repentance and accountability. Antonio and Sebastian, however, show little comparable remorse, which makes Prospero’s decision to extend mercy toward them more difficult for me to accept. I still think Prospero’s choice to forgive is important because it allows him to stop being controlled by the wrongs committed against him and to move forward with his life. At the same time, his mercy does not require him to pretend that nothing happened, since he openly confronts them with their crimes and regains what was wrongfully taken from him. For me, Shakespeare’s strongest lesson is that mercy should prevent justice from becoming revenge while still allowing wrongdoing to be recognized and addressed.

When I was living in Vancouver, my younger brother took an older Apple MacBook that belonged to me and brought it with him to school. I was especially upset when I discovered that he had not only taken it without permission but had later sold it to help pay for a newer computer. I had every reason to demand that he repay me or at least give me the money he received from selling something that was mine. After talking with my parents, however, I learned more about how much he was struggling to get through school and how badly he needed a reliable computer. Because he was my brother and I loved him, I eventually decided that preserving our relationship was more important than making sure I received exact repayment. I still told him that taking and selling my property without asking was not acceptable, so forgiving him did not require me to pretend that what happened was right. Even after he graduated, I never demanded money or another computer from him, although I probably would have willingly helped him sell the MacBook if he had simply asked me beforehand. That experience taught me that mercy can acknowledge a genuine wrong while choosing not to allow anger, punishment, or repayment to become more important than a relationship.

Hugh Nibley’s explanation that “Prospero” means to move forward, advance, and become better changes the way I look at Prospero’s final decision in the play. To “Prospero” in my own life, I want to become better at holding people accountable without allowing their mistakes to create permanent resentment in me. My experience with my brother taught me that forgiving someone does not require pretending that an injustice never occurred or refusing to establish appropriate boundaries afterward. Instead, I can recognize what happened, learn from it, and still choose mercy when punishment would accomplish little besides satisfying my own anger. Prospero eventually gives up his opportunity for revenge because continuing to punish his enemies would also keep him tied to the injuries of his past. In my own future relationships, including my family, community, and Church service, I want to be willing to correct wrongdoing while also giving people opportunities to repent, change, and move forward. I also need to remember that I will sometimes be the person who needs that same patience and mercy from others and, most importantly, from Jesus Christ. Following Christ therefore means continually progressing by learning from both justice and mercy, forgiving where I can, accepting correction when I am wrong, and refusing to let past injuries prevent me from becoming better.
